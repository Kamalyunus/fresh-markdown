What the business actually looks like

The previous tab is about how the population was built — which rows survive and why. This one is about what survived: how much of it there is, where the money sits, how much room to discount exists before the cost floor bites, and whether the data supports the structure the rest of the system assumes.

Every figure below is read live from reports/eda.json when this page is built, so nothing here can drift from the extract the way a typed number can. The full fifteen panels live in docs/eda.html; these are the ones that change a decision.

The money is concentrated, and that decides where to spend attention

Inventory Loss is currency, and the exploration budget is a currency amount. So the question "which SKUs matter" has a precise answer: the ones carrying the exposure. A deliberately non-optimal price on a tail SKU costs the same slice of budget as one on a head SKU and buys a fraction of the measurable evidence.

The curve below is cumulative COGS at risk — unit cost times the episode's whole supply, opening stock plus mid-window arrivals, counted once per episode — against SKUs ordered by it. The dashed diagonal is what a perfectly even business would look like. The gap between them is the whole point.

How much room there is before the cost floor

The agent may never price below cost, and that is structural rather than a rule it follows: the action set is built from d_max = 1 − cost/price, so a below-cost price is not expressible. What that costs is options. An episode whose cost ratio is high has few feasible tiers, and one with fewer than two cannot be experimented on at any price.

The deep entry arm is not always there

Entry offers five arms, from 15pp shallower than the category reference to one 5pp step deeper. The single deeper arm is the escape valve for the clearance/scrap trade — the only way the agent can choose to clear harder rather than protect margin. The cost floor removes it wherever the cost ratio is high, and until this panel existed nobody had measured how often.

Read this as a statement about capability, not about data quality. Where the deep arm is absent, the DP is choosing from a shorter menu, and the clearance figure it produces on those episodes is the best it could do rather than the best there is.

Does the hierarchy have anything to work with?

Three separate mechanisms fall back to a parent when a cell is too thin: dispersion (subcategory → category → global), level calibration (shrunk toward the parent by the evidence behind it), and the posterior cell map. Each has its own minimum. Whether those minimums are cleared decides whether the system is really operating at subcategory grain or has quietly collapsed to global.

A low share here is not a failure — the hierarchy is built for it, and a factor fit on a handful of rows is noise that gets projected onto prices. It is a fact about what grain the answers are really at.

And whether the level held still

The last one earns its place by history. The calibration factors are fit on one window and applied forward, and the fortnight originally chosen turned out to be the most anomalous stretch in five months — so the factors inherited the anomaly and pushed the gate the wrong way. It took a round of debugging to find. This plot would have shown it in a second.

None of this decides anything. It produces no gate, no threshold, and no value that gets pasted into the configuration — bootstrap.measure owns those, and a second source for one of them is exactly the kind of quiet disagreement this system has a checker for. What it does is make the population legible before any model is fit on it, which is the only point at which a surprise is cheap.
