The one number history could not give us

Price response is the quantity the whole system turns on, and it is the one thing we do not know. History cannot point-identify it — the legacy policy priced by the clock, so price and demand move together for reasons that have nothing to do with customers. So the prior does not pretend to a point estimate. It is the likelihood itself, read honestly: sharp where the data speaks, wide where it does not, and discarded where it points the wrong way.

First: what ε actually does

One exponent moves the demand model's answer from the reference price to any other price. It is the entire price mechanism in the system:

μ(d) = μ_ref · ( (1 − d) ⁄ (1 − d_ref) ) ^ ε ε constrained negative at d = 0 μ = 0.80 · (1.00 ⁄ 0.70) ^ −1 = 0.56 full price at d = 0.30 μ = 0.80 the reference itself at d = 0.60 μ = 0.80 · (0.40 ⁄ 0.70) ^ −1 = 1.40 at the cost floor

The ML model is consulted once per hour and that single number is swung across the whole price ladder by this exponent. Get ε wrong and every price the agent considers is mispriced — not by a little, and in a direction that compounds over a window.

Two confounds, two defences

Legacy history is confounded twice, and each confound gets its own control — neither substitutes for the other:

The whole curve, not its peak

Each arm scores every candidate ε on a grid over the feasible range with a censored Poisson likelihood — Poisson, because its estimate of the mean is trustworthy whatever the true noise shape, so no dispersion parameter enters and nothing here depends on any other fit:

for each candidate ε on a grid across [−4.00, −0.05]: μ_i(ε) = μ_ref,i · exp( ε · L_i ) L_i = log( (1 − d_i) ⁄ (1 − d_ref) ) log L(ε) = Σ log P( D = k_i | μ_i(ε) ) hours that did not sell out + Σ log P( D ≥ q_i | μ_i(ε) ) hours that did the PRIOR is the curve itself, not its argmax: w(ε) ∝ exp( log L(ε) ⁄ deff ) each arm, as a density own = ½ w_naive + ½ w_controlled the two-arm mixture

Keeping the curve is the difference between honesty and false precision. A previous version kept only each arm's peak — and a category whose likelihood was flat (no price variation at all) reported the same confident four-decimal number as one whose likelihood was sharp, because the argmax of a flat curve is just the edge of the grid. As a density, a flat likelihood is the uniform over the feasible range: the honest statement that the data said nothing.

Three guards, all measured

Why a fitted number would have been worse than an honest width

A confidently fitted ε makes the offline replay look excellent, because it fits the residual by construction. What it teaches the agent is the confound: hold price, burn scrap — the very behaviour the legacy rule already produces. The dashboard would smile while the system failed. A wide prior costs only patience; a confident wrong one takes weeks of bounded updates to walk back, across every cell at once.

What the agent launches with

Whatever each category's density says — its own measurement, the pooled density, or the uniform — the behavioural consequence is the same by design: conservative in the direction that matters. A wide prior keeps the DP holding price where it is unsure, because deepening only pays when |ε| clears a per-episode economic bar, and an unsure posterior rarely clears it.

That asymmetry is deliberate because clearance is not the problem. The business is not losing money for want of selling the stock; it is losing money on the terms of the sale. The cheapest mistake to recover from is holding price a little too long. Discounting too hard gives away margin that no later decision can win back — and production exploration, not history, is what closes the gap.
