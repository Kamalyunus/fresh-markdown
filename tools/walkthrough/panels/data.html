The population every other number is measured on

Before anything is modelled, the raw feed is reduced to a population we are willing to defend, through thirteen counted waterfall rows. Every stage is counted and written to the manifest — so any figure anywhere in this system traces back to the rows behind it.

Every stage is counted twice over: in rows and episodes, and in money — unit cost times the episode's whole supply, opening stock plus everything that arrived mid-window, once per episode. The two disagree, and the disagreement is what matters. A filter can remove one percent of the rows and fifteen percent of the exposure, and only the second number says whether what survives still looks like the business.

The drops are all structural — duplicate hour rows, windows split by holes in the feed, the exclusion window, out-of-range discounts, negative quantities, an inventory chain that contradicts itself between hours, null categories, and zero base prices. An hour that sells more than it opened with is not on that list: it is a restock — real stock arrived — and it is flagged, not dropped. Then a labelling pass marks what the planner cannot handle without removing it: a missing cost (a zero cost is missing, not free), a cost at or above the full price, an untrustworthy window counter, a window longer than 120 hours, and a restock in the final hour. Nothing is dropped silently, and nothing is dropped for being awkward to price.

The definition that had to be got right

An episode is one SKU in one facility across one clearance window — and it is deliberately not keyed by calendar date. Clearance windows routinely run past midnight. A date key would split one economic episode into two, reset the price ceiling halfway through, and charge the carried-over inventory to scrap at the seam — inventing a loss that never happened and hiding the decision that mattered.

Instead an episode is a maximal run of consecutive hours over which the source's own countdown decrements by exactly one per hour.

What gets removed, and what only gets labelled

A filter drops an episode only when its rows cannot be believed — negative quantities, an inventory chain whose hours contradict each other, two conflicting states for the same hour — or when it falls outside the period the study covers. Everything else is flagged and kept.

The distinction matters because the two populations answer different questions. The demand model never sees cost, the window counter, or the inventory chain, so an episode with a missing cost or a mid-window restock is an ordinary observation to it — and removing those episodes was taking more than 70% of the money at risk out of every model fit. What they break is the solver, so the solver reads a flag instead.

A restocked window is the clearest case. The hours themselves are honest — each one's sales were censored against the stock actually on the shelf at the time — so it trains the demand model, is replayed in the backtest (arrivals applied as an exogenous adjustment at the hour they landed), and its scrap and discount cost count toward Inventory Loss like any other episode's. The one restock that still gates is a restock in the final hour: it lands after the last decision, so no close state can be read for the episode at all.

Restocks are not a limitation of the agent. In production a restock is trivial — inventory is part of the state, the DP re-solves from scratch every hour, and a shelf that went from 2 units to 6 is simply a different starting position at the next decision. Nothing carries over that a restock would invalidate.
