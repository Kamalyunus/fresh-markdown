Every hour, the agent solves a small problem exactly

It is a Markov Decision Process, solved as a dynamic program. The decision itself is arithmetic rather than a model — every step of it is on this page, and it can be checked by hand. What makes it interesting is the one uncertain input: how many units will sell in the next hour, which arrives as a probability for every possible answer rather than as a single guess.

The problem, stated once

Formally this is a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process. Markov is a claim about what matters: the three numbers in the state below are a sufficient description of the situation, so given them, nothing else about how the episode arrived here changes the right price. Finite horizon is the expiry clock — the episode ends, and it ends at a known hour.

What it minimises

One number, in won. Not a ratio, not a score — the actual money the episode loses, which is additive across hours and so can be carried straight into the recursion with no transformation:

IL = Σ over hours ( P₀ − price ) × units sold ← discount given away + cost × units left at expiry ← scrap

Both terms are losses, so every figure on this page is negative and less negative is better. Reporting later divides IL by the full-price value of what sold to get IL%, but the agent never optimises that ratio — its denominator moves when the price moves, which makes it the wrong thing to hand an optimiser.

The state, the actions, and the recursion

state s = (a, q, t) a = discount in force, q = units on hand, t = hours left actions A(a) = { d on the 2.5pp grid : a ≤ d ≤ d_max } d_max = 1 − cost ⁄ P₀ V(a, q, 0) = − cost × q Q(a, q, t, d) = Σₖ P(D = k | μ(d), r) · [ −P₀·d·min(k, q) + V(d, q − min(k, q), t−1) ] V(a, q, t) = max over d ∈ A(a) of Q(a, q, t, d)

Read the middle line slowly, because the rest of this page is just that line worked by hand. For a candidate discount d, every possible sales count k is weighted by its probability. Each one settles money now — −P₀·d per unit actually sold — and leaves a different position for the next hour, whose value is already known. Note what sits in the first slot of that V: d, not a. The price you choose becomes the ceiling you inherit.

Only one term is not arithmetic: P(D = k), the chance of selling exactly k units. Where that comes from is the subject of section two.

Bellman’s equation — and why this one is solved exactly

Those last two lines are Bellman’s equation, the standard statement of any sequential decision under uncertainty: the value of a position is the best available combination of what you collect now and what you are worth afterwards. Writing it down is the easy part. Solving it as written usually is not — in most problems the states cannot be enumerated, so the equation is attacked approximately: sample part of it, fit a function to the rest, learn from experience and hope the estimate settles near the truth. That is what reinforcement learning is for, and it is the normal way this equation gets handled.

None of that is needed here. Inventory runs under thirty units, the horizon under twelve hours, and the ladder holds at most forty rungs — a state space small enough to write out in full. So we solve Bellman’s equation exactly, by dynamic programming, in a fraction of a second: no sampling, no function approximation, no training loop, and no convergence to take on faith. What the agent returns is the optimum for the inputs it was given, not an estimate of it — and the same inputs will return it again tomorrow.

Where the 25 rungs come from

The agent never picks a price freely. It picks a rung off a fixed ladder of 2.5-percentage-point steps, so every price it can ever quote is P₀ × (1 − k × 2.5%) — there is no continuous optimisation and no rounding afterwards. Two things decide how long that ladder is.

First the cost floor. The deepest legal discount is the one that reaches unit cost, and it is computed before any arithmetic runs, so a below-cost price is not evaluated and rejected — it is never constructed:

d_max = 1 − cost ⁄ P₀ = 1 − 4,000 ⁄ 10,000 = 60% off rungs = 60 ⁄ 2.5 + 1 = 25 (the “+ 1” is the 0% rung — holding) → 0%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, … , 55%, 57.5%, 60% prices → ₩10,000, ₩9,750, ₩9,500, ₩9,250, … , ₩4,500, ₩4,250, ₩4,000

Second, monotonicity: price may hold or deepen, never go back up. So the 25 rungs are the ladder at the start of the episode, when nothing has been discounted yet. Once a discount is in force, the rungs above it are gone for good — which is exactly why the discount carried in from the previous hour has to be part of the state rather than a note in a log.

solved 1st

Start at the end, where the answer is obvious

A dynamic program is solved backwards, because the last hour is the only one with no future to reason about. When the window closes, whatever is left is scrapped at cost. No decision, no uncertainty — just a bill.

Value of finishing with 0 units = ₩0 Value of finishing with 1 unit = −₩4,000 Value of finishing with 2 units = −₩8,000 Value of finishing with 3 units = −₩12,000

That is the whole terminal condition. Every other number on this page is built out of it by stepping backwards one hour at a time.

One hour earlier: a price buys a distribution, not a number

Now go back one hour. Three units, sixty minutes, and a price to pick. Demand arrives in two pieces, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The machine-learned model supplies a base demand — μ_ref, the average units this SKU moves in this hour at the category’s reference discount, here 0.80 at 30% off. That model is blind to price by construction; it is never asked what a different price would do. Moving off the reference is the job of a single parametric term, and the two together are what make the demand model semi-parametric:

μ(d) = μ_ref · ( (1 − d) ⁄ (1 − d_ref) ) ^ ε ε is the price response (constrained negative, at d = 0 μ = 0.80 · (1.00 ⁄ 0.70) ^ −1 = 0.56 measured at −1.0 today) at d = 0.30 μ = 0.80 ← the reference itself at d = 0.60 μ = 0.80 · (0.40 ⁄ 0.70) ^ −1 = 1.40

So the ML model is consulted once per hour, and that one number is then swung across all 25 rungs by the exponent — 0.56 at full price, 1.40 at the cost floor. Every price effect in this system lives in ε, which is the reason a single number is worth running experiments to learn.

But you never sell 0.56 of anything. What the agent needs is the chance of selling exactly none, exactly one, exactly two — and that is what the second frozen input is for. Alongside the average we measured how spread out hourly demand is around it: the dispersion. Average and spread together define the full distribution, and that distribution is what enters the arithmetic.

solved 2nd

Every outcome has a payoff and a destination

Take one candidate — 30% off, ₩7,000 — in that second-to-last hour. Each possible sales count does two things at once: it settles some money now, and it hands the agent a different starting position for the final hour. And the value of every final-hour position is already known, from the box above.

Weigh, add, then do it again for every rung

Multiply each outcome by its probability and add them up. That single number is the price’s score. Then repeat for all 25 rungs and keep the best. Below are two of them side by side — the 30% discount from the diagram, and simply holding at full price.

Holding wins by ₩1,402, and the two columns show exactly why. Discounting 30% lifts expected sales from 0.56 units to 0.80 — a real lift. But it hands ₩3,000 to every unit that sells, including the 0.56 units that were going to sell anyway. At these numbers the giveaway is larger than the rescue. That is not a rule anyone wrote down; it fell out of the arithmetic, and at a different cost, price or hour it comes out the other way.

solved 3rd → last

Now step back another hour, and another

The winning score for “3 units, 1 hour left” is −₩9,874. Write it down. It is now a known value, exactly like the scrap figures were — so the same fan can be drawn one hour further back, landing on it instead of on the scrap bill. Repeat until you reach the present.

The top-left cell, −₩4,881, is the answer: what this episode is expected to cost under the best available plan. It is 16% of the ₩30,000 of stock at stake, and it is the number the agent is minimising when it chooses a price.

Read the table across and the mechanism is visible without any mathematics. The same three units are worth −₩4,881 with four hours ahead of them and −₩12,000 with none. Time is the asset. The agent discounts when it is running out of time, not because the clock says so — which is precisely what the rule it replaces does.

Which hours — not just how many

The ML model does not hand over a single number for the episode. It predicts one base demand per remaining hour and passes the whole path, because hour of day is one of its features — a 7pm hour and a 3pm hour arrive carrying different μ_ref. The price-response term then swings each of those hours across the rungs independently.

The DP consumes that path element by element: the hour being priced uses the first entry, the hour after it the second, and the terminal scrap bill lands at the end of the last one. So “2 hours left” is never a quantity of time on its own — it is two specific hours, each with its own demand, and the agent knows which two.

Which is worth a great deal. Below, three episodes hold identical stock with an identical number of hours on the clock. Only the hours themselves differ:

The forecast path and the horizon have to describe the same window, and the system verifies that rather than trusting it: if the path does not carry exactly one entry per remaining hour, the decision is rejected and no price is returned at all. An episode whose window was truncated at a date boundary looks identical to a valid request in every other respect, and would quietly optimise the wrong stretch of time.

And in an hour, all of it is thrown away

Only the first decision is ever executed. Everything else the solve produced — the price it would set at every other position in that grid — is never committed to anything. It exists so the first decision can be priced correctly, and then it is discarded. An hour later the whole calculation runs again from the beginning.

That is not waste, it is the point. In the intervening hour reality picks a branch, and the agent finds out which one:

Three things change before the next solve, and each can move the answer. The inventory is whatever actually sold, not what was forecast. The horizon is one hour shorter, so every remaining unit has less time to find a buyer. And the anchor is the price just used, which permanently removes every rung above it.

A fourth thing changes quietly: the forecast itself is refreshed each hour, so the base demand feeding the next solve need not be the one that fed this one. A shopper surge, a quiet afternoon, a neighbouring store's promotion — none of that is predicted, and none of it needs to be. It shows up as a different state and a different forecast an hour later, and the agent re-derives its answer from whatever it finds.

This is why the agent never follows a plan. It holds no position between hours and carries no commitment forward. Ask it what it will do at 2 hours left and the honest answer is a question back: with how many units on hand? Everything on this page is recomputed, from scratch, sixty minutes from now.

How much arithmetic that is

Four hours × 25 price rungs × 4 inventory levels (3, 2, 1, 0) = 400 state–action values, each one a weighted sum over 26 possible sales counts — 10,400 terms for this episode. Those are terms of arithmetic, not routes through the window: the agent is not enumerating futures, it is filling in a small table. No search, no approximation, no training loop — every cell is computed, so the answer is exact and the same state always produces the same price.

The anchor does not multiply that. Every rung is scored once, and the value under a given anchor is a running maximum taken across those same scores — deepest rung upward — rather than a fresh enumeration per anchor. That is what makes the monotonicity constraint free rather than expensive. Real episodes carry more hours and more units and still finish inside 0.09 seconds at the 95th percentile, measured on production state in shadow mode. The hourly repricing budget has room to spare.
